A rice-straw boiler you can turn the dials on

Two 3D views of the same boiler, driven live by a heat-loss model built from its design workbook: the plant seen from outside, and the flow of gas, water and steam inside. Change a dial and both views, and every chart, change at once. At the design point the boiler is 81.3 percent efficient.

2. The plant from outside

Drag to turn, scroll to zoom. The fuel conveyor and the feed fan speed follow the load and the air, the sight glass glows with the flame, the stack lamp and plume show alarms, smoke and steam. Details like platforms and the filter are for scale and are illustrative.

3. Inside: gas, water and steam

The same boiler cut open. Hot flue gas (colour = temperature) passes the furnace, settling chamber, the 127-tube convection bank and the economizer, then leaves by the stack. Feed water (blue) is heated in the economizer, rises to the steam drum and leaves as steam (white). The furnace, settling chamber and bank temperatures are the workbook's iterated values; the stack temperature and air are yours.

5. Run a 12-hour shift

The fuel gets wetter and drier. A policy sets the excess air every 30 minutes and the 3D boiler follows. The score is the gap to the best possible setting.






Assumption, not measurement: the workbook has no penalty for too little air, so a stylised rule adds one: below a minimum air (35 % plus 0.15 % per kg of extra fuel water per tonne) the efficiency drops 0.25 points for every point of air missing. Under that rule the best setting at each step is exactly that minimum. Replace it with a plant curve before drawing conclusions.

  • The model reproduces the workbook's flue gas mass (6,958 kg per tonne of fuel) and its oxygen share (7.77 %).
  • It is a design calculation, not plant data. The unburnt and radiation losses are fixed values from the workbook; the 3D plant details (platforms, filter) are illustrative.
  • An earlier machine-learning test found nothing better than a straight line, because the data came from a straight-line formula. That is why the heat-loss model is the core.
